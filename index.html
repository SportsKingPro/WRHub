How the score is built

The score is the sum of four factors, each rated 0–3 in half-point steps, so it runs from 0 to 12 and is shown on a 1–12 scale (anything under 1 reads as 1). The same rubric is applied to every historical benchmark and to each date in 2026, so the numbers are comparable. Bands: Calm below 4, Tense 4–6.9, Crisis 7–9.9, Brink 10 and above.

US combat involvement: 0 none; 1 limited operations, strikes or advisers; 2 sustained regional war with US forces; 3 total war, or war against a peer.
Great-power confrontation: 0 none; 1 competition (sanctions, proxies, drills, arms races); 2 direct crisis or standoff between nuclear-armed powers; 3 direct combat between great powers, or imminent.
Nuclear danger: 0 none; 1 nuclear rhetoric or eroding arms control; 2 explicit nuclear threats or raised alert levels tied to a live crisis; 3 nuclear use, or launch posture imminent.
Homeland exposure: 0 none; 1 background terrorism, cyber or economic exposure; 2 official threat advisories, confirmed attacks on US systems or sustained disruption; 3 mass-casualty attack on the homeland, or an imminent threat to it. Half points sit between the descriptions.

What the scale does and does not measure: it rates the risk of wider war and danger to the homeland, not the death toll of any single event, which is why September 11 scores below the Cold War peaks. In practice the floor is about 3, because nuclear arsenals and background terror and cyber exposure never disappear, and no historical case reaches 11–12, which would mean nuclear war is underway. The benchmark scores are my judgments from the historical record; the reasoning is shown so you can disagree with it. If the score is 8 or above the guide below recommends Tier 2; from 5 to 7.5 it suggests reviewing Tier 2; under 5, Tier 1 only.
